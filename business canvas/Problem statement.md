## Problem statement

Informal traders and spaza-shop owners in South Africa operate important businesses but often lack simple tools for recording sales, monitoring stock, and tracking business performance.

Many rely on memory, notebooks, or informal methods to manage daily transactions. As a result:

- They may not know their true daily revenue or profit.
- They struggle to identify which products are selling quickly.
- Stock shortages and poor stock control can lead to lost sales.
- They lack reliable financial records when applying for funding or supplier support.
- Banks, distributors, and Enterprise Supplier Development programmes have limited verified information about the businesses they want to support.

Research has found that many informal businesses do not keep financial records, while stock control and access to finance remain significant challenges.  In addition, digital adoption can be difficult because of data costs, limited digital skills, language barriers, unreliable connectivity, and limited access to smartphones. However, for traders who do have smartphones, WhatsApp is often a preferred digital channel.[^1][^2][^3]

### Formal version for the business canvas

> Informal traders and spaza-shop owners often lack accessible and user-friendly tools to record sales, manage inventory, and track business performance. This limits their ability to make informed decisions, avoid stock-outs, demonstrate business activity, and access funding or supplier opportunities. Existing accounting and inventory systems can be too complex, expensive, or difficult to use, especially for traders with limited time, digital skills, or connectivity.

### Short version for a presentation slide

> Township traders run active businesses but often lack simple, reliable ways to record sales and manage stock. This leads to poor business visibility, stock-outs, and limited access to funding and supplier opportunities.

### The underlying issue

The central issue is not simply that traders lack accounting software. The deeper problem is:

> **Their daily economic activity is not being captured in a simple, trusted, and useful way.**

Because transactions are not consistently recorded:

1. The trader has limited visibility into the business.
2. The business cannot easily prove its performance.
3. Funders and suppliers cannot confidently assess the business.
4. Opportunities for growth, credit, and formal support are missed.

## Problem framing

### Who is affected?

- Spaza-shop owners.
- Informal food sellers.
- Township retailers.
- Small businesses that rely on cash or informal records.
- Distributors and funders trying to support these businesses.


### What causes the problem?

- Manual recordkeeping.
- Limited financial-management skills.
- Complex accounting applications.
- Language and digital-literacy barriers.
- Cost of data and devices.
- Unreliable connectivity or electricity.
- Limited trust in formal financial systems.


### What are the consequences?

- Poor understanding of profit and cash flow.
- Inaccurate stock information.
- Lost sales due to unavailable products.
- Difficulty qualifying for finance.
- Weak evidence for ESD or supplier programmes.
- Limited visibility for distributors.
- Slower business growth.


## Link to KasiPilot

KasiPilot addresses this problem by allowing traders to record business activity through a familiar conversational channel.

For example:

> “I sold four kotas at R35 each.”

Instead of requiring the trader to complete a form, KasiPilot converts the message into a structured transaction and updates the trader’s records.

The solution therefore targets the root cause: **the difficulty of capturing business information consistently and conveniently**. It also recognises that digital tools must be designed around the realities of township businesses rather than expecting traders to adapt to complicated software.

## Strong final version

> South Africa’s informal traders and spaza-shop owners contribute significantly to the economy, yet many still manage sales and stock using memory, paper, or fragmented methods. This makes it difficult to understand business performance, prevent stock shortages, and provide reliable evidence when seeking funding or supplier support. Existing digital tools are often too complex, costly, or inaccessible for users with limited digital skills, connectivity, or time. KasiPilot addresses this gap by providing a WhatsApp-based, voice- and text-enabled business assistant that turns everyday trader conversations into useful sales, inventory, and business records.
